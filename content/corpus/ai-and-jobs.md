# AI and jobs: our earlier group project

Before this project, our class looked at how AI might change employment, using three sources.

Ben Casselman (2026) reports in The New York Times that public debate has focused on programmers and tech workers, who have faced high-profile layoffs, but that many economists are more worried about a larger group: customer service representatives, bookkeepers, payroll clerks and human resources specialists. These back-office jobs are spread across many firms and are an obvious place for companies to cut costs with AI. Casselman stresses that, for now, this is a fear and not a forecast, because there is little firm evidence that AI has hurt the labour market as a whole.

Johansson, Madgavkar and Schrader (2026) at McKinsey & Company measure exposure to automation by skill rather than by job. Work will increasingly mean collaboration between humans, AI agents and robots. Human capabilities such as empathy and leadership are the least likely to be affected, while technical and repetitive tasks, such as invoicing and operating machinery, are much more exposed.

Vlad Tenev (2025), speaking at TED, argues that alongside the idea of an intelligence explosion, the data show rapidly accelerating job creation, which he calls a job singularity. He predicts new kinds of work that are hard to imagine today, including micro-corporations and single-person companies, and says future jobs may look like leisure to us.

Our group's conclusion was that routine tasks, not whole degrees, are most at risk, and that people who build skills that cannot be reduced to a procedure can use AI as a tool rather than be replaced by it.
