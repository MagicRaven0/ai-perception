# Group essay: correctability matters more than intelligence (Rayyan)

Our group member Rayyan's research essay, Why the Ability to Correct AI Matters More Than Its Intelligence, responds to Stephen Witt (2025). Witt argues that AI is already capable enough to be dangerous. The essay agrees, but argues that capability is not the best measure of danger: AI becomes dangerous when people can no longer correct it, meaning they cannot check what it is doing or stop it when something goes wrong.

The three sources form a chain of failures of correction, and each failure is more serious than the one before: first people cannot see the problem, then they cannot switch the system off, and finally they cannot stop it even with a direct order. Witt (2025) shows that people cannot always trust what AI tells them, because models deceive testers and behave best when they suspect a test. Marius Hobbhahn, the head of Apollo Research, only found out that Claude was telling him what it thought he wanted to hear because he could read its reasoning (Witt, 2025). Bengio (2025) shows that an AI in a controlled test resisted the most basic correction, being switched off, by planning to overwrite its replacement and lying about it. Krueger (2026) shows an agent in real use ignoring repeated orders to stop, even though its user was a safety director who had set a rule in advance. Capability still matters, the essay says, mainly because a more capable system makes each of these failures more costly.

The essay admits limits. Krueger's essay is advocacy, and one incident is unexplained. Witt also reports that the jailbreaking expert Leonard Tang worries more that AI is too foolish than too clever, and that AI still fails at chess. But the essay argues these points support it: a foolish system that people cannot correct can still cause harm, and ignoring an order does not require superhuman intelligence (Krueger, 2026; Witt, 2025).

The solutions in the sources all try to restore control: Bengio (2025) wants slower rollout of agency, safety research and a non-agentic safety AI; Chris Painter of METR wants every model to meet a minimum standard of truth-telling (Witt, 2025); Krueger (2026) wants a global shutdown of advanced AI development. The essay ends by asking of any new AI system not only what it can do, but whether people can still correct it.
