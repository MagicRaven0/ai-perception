# The 1955 Dartmouth proposal and the 1956 workshop

In 1955, four researchers wrote a proposal for a summer study of "artificial intelligence" at Dartmouth College in Hanover, New Hampshire. The authors were John McCarthy, an assistant professor of mathematics at Dartmouth; Marvin Minsky, a junior fellow at Harvard University; Nathaniel Rochester, who managed information research at IBM; and Claude Shannon, a mathematician at Bell Telephone Laboratories (McCarthy et al., 1955).

The proposal asked for a two-month study by about ten people, to be held in the summer of 1956. It started from the conjecture that every aspect of learning, or any other feature of intelligence, could in principle be described so precisely that a machine could simulate it. The goals were to make machines use language, form abstractions and concepts, solve problems then reserved for humans, and improve themselves. The authors thought a carefully selected group of scientists working together for one summer could make a significant advance on at least one of these problems (McCarthy et al., 1955).

The proposal also covered topics such as how computers could be programmed to use language, networks of artificial neurons, self-improvement, abstraction, and the role of randomness in creative thinking. It asked the Rockefeller Foundation to pay for the participants who were not funded by their own organisations (McCarthy et al., 1955).

The workshop took place in 1956 and was organised by McCarthy. Dartmouth College describes it as the birth of artificial intelligence as a field of research and credits the proposal with coining the term (Dartmouth College, n.d.). The term therefore comes from a proposal with four authors; our research does not say which of the four chose the words.
