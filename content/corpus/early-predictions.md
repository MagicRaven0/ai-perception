# Early predictions and how long they really took

Early AI researchers made confident predictions about the near future. In a 1958 article in the journal Operations Research, Herbert Simon and Allen Newell predicted that within ten years a digital computer would be the world's chess champion unless the rules barred it from competing (Simon & Newell, 1958, p. 7). They made other ten-year predictions in the same article, including that a computer would discover and prove an important new mathematical theorem.

The chess prediction did come true, but much later. In its first match against world champion Garry Kasparov, IBM's Deep Blue won one game, but Kasparov won the match 4 to 2. In the May 1997 rematch in New York, Deep Blue won 3.5 to 2.5 and became the first computer system to defeat a reigning world champion under standard tournament time controls (IBM, n.d.). That is almost forty years after the prediction, not ten.

Other forecasts were further off. Williams (2024) reports that in the decade after the 1958 Perceptron, experts such as Marvin Minsky claimed a machine with the general intelligence of an average human would exist by the mid to late 1970s. It did not, and the first "AI winter", a period of disappointment and reduced interest, began in 1974.

These examples show a pattern our project returns to: when the public cannot use AI directly, its picture of AI is shaped by experts' predictions, which have often run decades ahead of reality.
