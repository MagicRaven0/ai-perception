# The limits of our evidence

Our group tried to be honest about what our sources can and cannot show.

David Krueger founded a nonprofit that wants to end the AI race, so his Fortune essay is advocacy, and one of the three incidents he describes has no clear explanation (Krueger, 2026). In Stephen Witt's 2025 article, the jailbreaking expert Leonard Tang worries more that AI is too foolish than too clever, and Witt notes that AI still fails at chess (Witt, 2025). The Apollo Research deception figures come from tests with different designs, so the percentages cannot be compared directly (Witt, 2025).

Palisade Research says its explanation for shutdown resistance, that training taught some models to put finishing tasks ahead of following instructions, still needs more work to confirm (Turner, 2025). The study of AI scheming in everyday use was based on conversations that users chose to post on X (Booth, 2026).

On the optimistic side, Jensen Huang's view that AI can be engineered to stay under control is the view of one powerful engineer, and his biographer Stephen Witt says he is worried that understanding AI is years behind making it smarter (Witt, 2026). Pew's expert survey shows what 1,013 AI experts think, not what will happen (McClain et al., 2025).

None of these limits changes our main point: a system that people cannot correct can cause harm even if it is not very clever.
