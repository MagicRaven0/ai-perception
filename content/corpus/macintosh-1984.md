# The Macintosh and the friendly interface

When the Macintosh was being finished in January 1984, computers were seen as complex and difficult to use. The Macintosh aimed to be the first mass-market personal computer that was truly user-friendly. It built on ideas from others, including former Apple manager Jef Raskin and researchers at Xerox PARC and the Stanford Research Institute (Steve Jobs Archive, 2024).

Steve Jobs had managed the Macintosh team since 1981. He believed that a team of engineers, designers and programmers who were also artists and musicians could make a machine for everyday people, "a computer for the rest of us". He asked team members to sign their work like artists, while reminding them they were building a tool for other people to use (Steve Jobs Archive, 2024).

Several team members shaped how friendly the machine felt. Susan Kare designed the system's fonts and icons, and her smiling Mac logo made the computer feel almost human. Bill Atkinson's graphics software was key to making it easy to use, and Andy Hertzfeld was a main architect of its operating system (Steve Jobs Archive, 2024).

Jobs told the team that within five years people would be using the Macintosh for things they had never imagined. In 2007 he remembered the team agreeing, the week before launch, that every computer would come to work this way (Steve Jobs Archive, 2024). For our argument, the Macintosh marks the moment computers were designed so that ordinary people felt in control of them.
