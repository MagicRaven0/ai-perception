# The 1958 Perceptron report and the AI winters

In July 1958, The New York Times published a short story about the Perceptron, a learning computer. The story cited the U.S. Navy as saying that the Perceptron would lead to machines able to walk, talk, see, write, reproduce themselves and be conscious of their own existence (Williams, 2024).

Williams (2024) argues that AI has moved through a cycle of booms and busts ever since. After the Perceptron, experts predicted human-level machines by the 1970s. When those systems turned out to know nothing about their subject matter and could not handle the ambiguity of everyday language, disappointment set in, and the first "AI winter" hit in 1974 after the perceived failure of the Perceptron.

By 1980 AI was popular again, in a boom built on expert systems: programs designed for one area of knowledge that could, for example, identify objects or diagnose diseases from observed data. There were also early driverless cars and robots that could read and play music. But in 1987 a second AI winter arrived, because expert systems could not handle new information they had not been built for (Williams, 2024).

In the 1990s researchers moved to data-driven machine learning and returned to neural networks, helped by the web and by more computing power (Williams, 2024). Williams (2024) warns that today's claims about humanlike AI echo the promises of 1958, and that many of the old problems remain.
