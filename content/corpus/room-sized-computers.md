# Computers in the 1950s: big machines, small capacity

The researchers of the 1950s were optimistic about ideas, not about hardware. Their own 1955 proposal says that the speed and memory of existing computers may not be enough to simulate many of the higher functions of the human brain. It argues, however, that the main obstacle was not the machines but the lack of programs that could make full use of them (McCarthy et al., 1955).

Computers of the period were large and rare. The Perceptron, introduced in 1958 and invented by Frank Rosenblatt, was a room-size analog computer that learned to predict which of two categories an image belonged to. When it gave a wrong answer, it changed its connections so it could do better next time, which is the same basic idea behind today's neural networks, only on a much smaller scale (Williams, 2024).

Because only universities, large companies such as IBM and Bell Labs, and the military had computers, almost nobody in the public used one. People learned about AI from what researchers and newspapers said it would do in the future. In our project's argument, this is why the 1950s public saw AI as a distant and impressive promise: they had no direct experience of the machines and no control over them (group commentary based on McCarthy et al., 1955, and Williams, 2024).
