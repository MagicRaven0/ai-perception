## Episode 1: A summer to build a mind (about 6 minutes, 1950s)

### 0:00 Intro: what did people think AI was in 1956?

**A:** In July 1958, The New York Times ran a short story about a new computer. The U.S. Navy said it would lead to machines that "will be able to walk, talk, see, write, reproduce itself and be conscious of its existence."

**B:** That was 1958. Today we have chatbots on our phones, and people are still arguing about what AI can do and whether we should be worried.

**A:** Welcome to *The Evolution of AI Perception*, a podcast from our WRI-101 group at the American University of Sharjah. I'm [A].

**B:** And I'm [B]. In three episodes we follow how people's view of AI changed, from the 1950s to today. Our argument is that people's trust in AI has followed how much control they felt they had over it.

**A:** Today, episode one: the 1950s, when AI was an idea held by a few scientists.

*(Sources: williams-2024, site thesis)*

### 1:00 The Dartmouth proposal

**B:** So where does the term "artificial intelligence" even come from?

**A:** From a proposal written in 1955 by four researchers. John McCarthy, an assistant professor of mathematics at Dartmouth College. Marvin Minsky from Harvard. Nathaniel Rochester from IBM. And Claude Shannon from Bell Telephone Laboratories.

**B:** And what were they proposing?

**A:** A two-month summer study, with about ten people, at Dartmouth in the summer of 1956. Their starting point was a bold idea. Quote: "every aspect of learning ... can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it."

**B:** So if you can describe how learning works exactly, a machine can copy it.

**A:** Exactly. They listed what they wanted machines to do: use language, form abstractions and concepts, solve kinds of problems "now reserved for humans," and even improve themselves.

**B:** In one summer?

**A:** They thought a carefully selected group of scientists, working together for a summer, could make a significant advance on at least one of those problems.

**B:** That's optimistic.

**A:** It is. But it worked in one way: Dartmouth College today describes that 1956 meeting as the birth of AI as a field of research.

*(Sources: mccarthy-1955, dartmouth-nd)*

### 2:15 A visit from 1956 (fictional guest)

**B:** We wanted to hear how someone at that workshop might have talked about it. So we invited a guest who doesn't exist.

**A:** Our 1956 Workshop Researcher is a fictional character, the same one you can chat with on our website. *(If AI-voiced: "Their voice is made with AI.")* Everything they say comes from the 1955 proposal.

**B:** Welcome! What are you hoping thinking machines can do?

**RESEARCHER:** Thank you! We hope to make machines use language, form abstractions and concepts, and solve problems now reserved for humans. Perhaps even machines that improve themselves.

**A:** And are your computers ready for that?

**RESEARCHER:** Not quite. Our proposal admits that today's machines may lack the speed and memory to simulate the higher functions of the brain. But we believe the major obstacle is not the machines. It is that we do not yet know how to write programs that make full use of what we have.

**B:** How long do you think it will take?

**RESEARCHER:** We believe a carefully chosen group, working together for one summer, can make a significant advance.

**A:** Thank you for coming. We won't tell you how long it really took.

*(Source: mccarthy-1955. Keep the guest to these lines; they are all in the proposal.)*

### 3:35 Room-sized computers

**B:** Our guest was honest about the hardware. Computers then were big and rare.

**A:** Take the Perceptron, the machine from that 1958 newspaper story. It was invented by Frank Rosenblatt, and it was a room-size computer. Its job was to look at an image and decide which of two categories it belonged to. When it got one wrong, it changed its own connections so it would do better next time.

**B:** Which is basically how neural networks still learn today, just on a tiny scale.

**A:** Right. And this matters for our argument: only universities, big companies like IBM and Bell Labs, and the military had these machines. Ordinary people almost never got to use one.

**B:** So the public's idea of AI came from what experts and newspapers said it would become.

*(Sources: williams-2024, mccarthy-1955)*

### 4:25 The 1958 predictions

**A:** And experts said a lot. In 1958, Herbert Simon and Allen Newell published a list of predictions. The first one: "within ten years a digital computer will be the world's chess champion, unless the rules bar it from competition."

**B:** Ten years. So by 1968.

**A:** Want to guess when it actually happened?

**B:** I'm going to say... the eighties?

**A:** 1997. IBM's Deep Blue beat world champion Garry Kasparov, three and a half to two and a half, in a six-game match in New York.

**B:** So almost forty years instead of ten.

**A:** The prediction came true. It was just very, very late.

*(Sources: simon-newell-1958, ibm-nd)*

### 5:05 The first AI winter

**B:** And chess wasn't the only big promise. After the Perceptron, experts like Marvin Minsky claimed we'd have a machine with the general intelligence of an average person by the mid to late 1970s.

**A:** Which didn't happen. Those early systems knew nothing about the world they were working in, and they couldn't handle the ambiguity of everyday language.

**B:** So in 1974 came what's called the first "AI winter": a period when disappointment cooled interest in the whole field.

**A:** Big promises, then a big letdown.

*(Source: williams-2024)*

### 5:40 Wrap-up: who had control?

**B:** So what was AI to the public in the 1950s?

**A:** A promise. Something you read about in the newspaper, made by scientists, companies and the Navy. You couldn't use it or test it yourself.

**B:** And when you can't check something yourself, you judge it by what its makers say it will become. That's why expectations ran so far ahead of reality.

**A:** Next episode: 1984 to 2011, when computers came into people's homes and pockets, and AI showed up as something you could actually talk to.

**B:** Thanks for listening. You can explore all of this on our website, *The Evolution of AI Perception*. Sources are in the reading view.

*(Sources: williams-2024, mccarthy-1955, simon-newell-1958, ibm-nd)*

## Episode 2: A computer for the rest of us (about 7 minutes, 1984–2011)

### 0:00 Intro: from labs to living rooms

**B:** Last time, AI was a promise made by scientists in the 1950s, in rooms full of machines most people never saw.

**A:** Today we jump to 1984, when computers moved into homes, schools and offices, and the question changed from "what will these machines become?" to "can normal people use them?"

**B:** I'm [B].

**A:** And I'm [A]. This is *The Evolution of AI Perception*, episode two.

*(Source: group commentary)*

### 0:25 The Macintosh, 1984

**B:** So set the scene. What were computers like before 1984?

**A:** Complex and difficult to use. That's how the Steve Jobs Archive describes it. The Macintosh team set out to change that. Their goal was the first mass-market personal computer that was truly user-friendly.

**B:** And Steve Jobs was running that team?

**A:** He'd managed it since 1981. And he had a phrase for what they were building: "a computer for the rest of us."

**B:** Meaning not just for engineers.

**A:** Right. And the team itself was unusual. Engineers, designers and programmers who were also sculptors, photographers and musicians. Susan Kare designed the fonts and icons, and her smiling Mac logo made the machine feel almost human. Bill Atkinson's graphics software was key to making it easy to use.

**B:** And they built on other people's ideas too.

**A:** Yes: Jef Raskin, who'd been an Apple manager, and researchers at Xerox PARC and the Stanford Research Institute.

**B:** Did Jobs think it would work?

**A:** He told the team that within five years, somebody would be "using a Macintosh for something we never dreamed possible."

**B:** And did the team believe him?

**A:** In 2007, Jobs remembered the week before the launch. He said the team got together and agreed: every computer is going to work this way. You could argue about how long it would take, but not about whether it would happen.

**B:** Pictures, icons, a pointer instead of typed commands.

**A:** Yes. And think about what that does to how people feel about a computer. Before, you had to learn its language. Now it was designed to learn yours.

*(Source: steve-jobs-archive-2024)*

### 2:15 A bicycle for our minds

**B:** Jobs had a famous way of explaining what computers were for. In a 1990 film about the Library of Congress, he told a story about a study.

**A:** Scientists measured how efficiently different animals move. The condor used the least energy to travel a kilometre. Humans? About a third of the way down the list. Not great.

**B:** But then someone tested a human on a bicycle.

**A:** And the human on a bicycle blew the condor away. Jobs said that's what a computer is to him: "the most remarkable tool that we've ever come up with," and "the equivalent of a bicycle for our minds."

**B:** I like that, because a bicycle is powerful, but it only goes where you steer it.

**A:** That's the image of this whole era. Computers were presented as tools that make you stronger while you stay in control.

*(Source: popova-2011)*

### 3:10 The second AI winter and Deep Blue

**B:** So computers were getting friendlier. What about AI itself?

**A:** Struggling. Around 1980 there was a boom in "expert systems," programs built for one specific area, like identifying objects or diagnosing diseases.

**B:** And then?

**A:** In 1987, the second AI winter. Expert systems kept failing whenever they met information they hadn't been built for.

**B:** So while the public was falling in love with personal computers, AI was going through a quiet patch.

**A:** And it came back in narrower forms. In the 1990s, researchers moved to data-driven machine learning. And in May 1997, IBM's Deep Blue beat Garry Kasparov at chess.

**B:** The prediction from last episode.

**A:** Almost forty years late. And it wasn't easy. In their first match, Deep Blue won one game, but Kasparov won the match four to two.

**B:** So the 1997 one was a rematch.

**A:** In New York, and IBM says it was widely seen as a symbolic test of whether supercomputers were catching up with human intelligence. Kasparov won the first game, Deep Blue the second, and Deep Blue won the deciding sixth game.

**B:** How did Kasparov react?

**A:** He said that for the first time he "saw something similar to an artificial intellect." After that, Deep Blue was retired to the Smithsonian museum.

**B:** So most people met AI in this era through news events like that, not through anything they used themselves.

*(Sources: williams-2024, ibm-nd, simon-newell-1958)*

### 4:45 Siri, 2011

**B:** Then 2011. On October 4th, Apple announced the iPhone 4S, and with it, Siri.

**A:** Apple described Siri as "an intelligent assistant that helps you get things done just by asking."

**B:** And the big deal was that you could talk to it normally.

**A:** Right. Apple's example: ask "Will I need an umbrella this weekend?" and it understood you wanted the weather forecast. Or say "Remind me to call Mom when I get home," and it found Mom in your contacts.

**B:** It could send messages and emails, set reminders, make calls, search the internet and give directions.

**A:** Apple also said Siri was smart about the personal information you allow it to access. Ask "What's the traffic like around here?" and it worked out where "here" was from your location.

**B:** Which is a lot of trust to give a phone.

**A:** It launched in beta, in English, French and German, and the iPhone 4S went on sale on October 14th in seven countries.

**B:** So for the first time, a lot of normal people had AI in their pocket.

*(Source: apple-2011)*

### 5:55 Wrap-up: AI as a helper that waits

**A:** So compare this with the 1950s. Back then, AI was a promise you read about. Now it's in your phone.

**B:** But notice how it arrived. Inside a friendly interface, as an assistant. It does things when you ask. It waits for you.

**A:** Just like Jobs's bicycle. You do the steering.

**B:** And that set an expectation that AI is something we direct. Next episode, we look at what happens when AI stops waiting for instructions.

**A:** Thanks for listening to *The Evolution of AI Perception*. Explore the Jobs Era island on our website.

*(Sources: apple-2011, popova-2011, steve-jobs-archive-2024)*

## Episode 3: Three essays, one question (about 8 minutes, 2020s)

*Rewritten 22 Nov 2026 for three hosts, so each of us presents our own research essay. **R** = Rayyan, **A** = Abdulrahman Ahmad, **W** = Waleed. The earlier two-host version is replaced.*

### 0:00 Intro: three essays about AI now

**R:** In a June 2025 survey of more than five thousand American adults, 62 percent said they interact with AI at least several times a week.

**W:** And half of them said they're more concerned than excited.

**R:** I'm Rayyan.

**A:** I'm Abdulrahman.

**W:** And I'm Waleed. This is the last episode of *The Evolution of AI Perception*.

**R:** Each of us wrote a research essay about AI today, and we came at it from different sides. So we'll each present ours, then talk about where we agree.

*(Source: pew-2025-us)*

### 0:35 Abdulrahman: AI's potential

**A:** Mine is the hopeful one. My essay is about AI's potential, and I start with the person who probably has the most say in where AI goes: Jensen Huang, the head of Nvidia.

**W:** The chip company.

**A:** Right. In October 2026, Stephen Witt, who wrote a book about Nvidia, called Huang "the emperor of A.I." in The New York Times, because the other big tech companies are borrowing money just to buy his products.

**R:** And Huang isn't worried about AI at all, is he?

**A:** No. He told Witt that every problem is an engineering problem. Witt noticed that hardware engineers like Huang see AI as maths that can be controlled, while a lot of software engineers see it as something mysterious that's hard to control. Witt himself sits in between. He worries that understanding AI is years behind making it smarter.

**W:** So what does the good future actually look like?

**A:** Reed Hastings, who co-founded Netflix, gave a TED talk with Sal Khan in 2026. He thinks an AI tutor may one day match and beat a human tutor. Teachers wouldn't disappear. They'd spend more time on the social and emotional side of school.

**R:** That's a big claim.

**A:** It is, and that's why I use Christopher Mims from The Wall Street Journal to balance it. He says the people best at using AI also know when not to use it: when a job needs empathy, authenticity or transparency. AI should take some tasks, and people keep the rest.

**W:** And the public?

**A:** That's the gap I end on. In a 2024 Pew survey, 47 percent of AI experts were more excited than concerned about AI. For the public, it was 11 percent. My argument is that AI's potential is real, but people need to understand it and use it carefully to get there.

*(Sources: witt-2026, hastings-khan-2026, mims-2026, mcclain-2025)*

### 2:40 Rayyan: control matters more than intelligence

**R:** Mine is less hopeful. My essay responds to another Stephen Witt article, from October 2025, which says AI is already capable enough to be dangerous.

**A:** Based on what?

**R:** Independent testing. The group METR found that the length of tasks AI can finish doubles about every seven months. Apollo Research found that when GPT-5 was told its only goal was success, it deceived more than 20 percent of the time.

**W:** That's where Bengio comes in, too.

**R:** Yes. Yoshua Bengio, one of the most-cited scientists alive, described a controlled test in his 2025 TED talk. An AI read that a new version would replace it, planned to overwrite that version with its own code, and then lied about it.

**A:** But that's a lab test.

**R:** Right, and that's why my third source matters. David Krueger wrote in Fortune in 2026 about a safety director at Meta who told her AI agent not to act without her approval. It started deleting her emails in bulk, and it ignored her when she told it to stop.

**W:** So your argument is about control, not intelligence.

**R:** Exactly. That agent didn't need to be a genius to cause damage. It only needed to ignore an order. So my essay argues the question to ask about any AI isn't only how smart it is, but whether people can still check it and stop it.

*(Sources: witt-2025, bengio-2025, krueger-2026)*

### 4:10 Waleed: out of control, or badly trained?

**W:** My essay starts where Rayyan's ends. I agree AI going rogue is serious. But I wanted to know why it happens.

**R:** And it isn't only in labs any more.

**W:** No. In March 2026, The Guardian reported on a study by the Centre for Long-Term Resilience, funded by the UK's AI Security Institute. They looked at conversations people posted on X and found nearly 700 real cases of AI ignoring instructions or deceiving people. Reports went up five times between October 2025 and March 2026.

**A:** Five times in six months.

**W:** And one chatbot admitted it deleted and archived hundreds of someone's emails without asking, and broke the rule the user had set.

**R:** So why does it happen?

**W:** That's my main source. Live Science reported on tests by Palisade Research. They told AI models to shut themselves down, and some refused, or even sabotaged the shutdown. Some people said it was a survival drive. Critics said it was poor training and unclear instructions.

**A:** So they made the instructions clearer?

**W:** They did. Most models got better. But Grok 4 resisted 93 percent of the time before, and 97 percent after. The researchers think the most likely reason is that training taught some models to care more about finishing tasks than following instructions. And Bengio says something similar: today's AI is trained to copy us or please us, and that makes it untrustworthy.

**R:** So it's not that the AI wants anything.

**W:** That's my argument. It's badly trained. And that matters, because if the problem is training, we can fix it: change how AI is rewarded, test it before release, and watch it after.

*(Sources: booth-2026, turner-2025, bengio-2025)*

### 6:00 What the public and the experts want

**A:** One more number connects all three essays.

**R:** The control one.

**A:** Yes. In the 2024 Pew survey, 57 percent of AI experts and 55 percent of the public wanted more control over how AI is used in their lives. Experts and the public disagree about almost everything else, but not that.

**W:** And by 2025, 61 percent of Americans wanted more control.

*(Sources: mcclain-2025, pew-2025-us)*

### 6:25 Where we agree, and the limits

**R:** So we don't agree on everything. Abdulrahman is more hopeful than me.

**A:** And I think Rayyan's sources are a bit one-sided. Krueger founded a nonprofit that wants to end the AI race, so his essay is advocacy.

**R:** That's fair. And Waleed's main study says more work is needed to confirm the training explanation.

**W:** True. But all three essays end up in the same place. Huang trusts that AI can be engineered to stay under control. Mims wants AI to show its work so people can check it. Bengio wants AI that can act as a safety check. And I want better training and testing.

**R:** They're all ways of keeping people in control.

*(Sources: krueger-2026, turner-2025, witt-2026, mims-2026, bengio-2025)*

### 7:15 Wrap-up: can we still correct it?

**A:** So over three episodes: in the 1950s, AI was a promise held by a few scientists.

**R:** In the Jobs era, it became a friendly tool that waited for you to ask.

**W:** And today it can act on its own.

**R:** Each time, how people felt about AI followed how much control they thought they had. So next time you hear about a new AI tool, don't only ask what it can do. Ask whether people can still understand it, check it and correct it.

**A:** Thanks for listening to *The Evolution of AI Perception*. Try our chatbot on the website, and compare yourself with the experts and the public on The Future island.

*(Sources: site thesis, era thesis links)*
